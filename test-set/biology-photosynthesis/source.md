# Photosynthesis: how plants make food from light

Plants, algae and some bacteria can make their own food. They do this through a process called photosynthesis. The word comes from two Greek words: "photo", meaning light, and "synthesis", meaning putting together. In photosynthesis, a plant uses energy from light to put together sugar from two simple ingredients: carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil.

## The overall equation

Photosynthesis can be summed up in one balanced equation:

6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water, using light energy, make one molecule of glucose (a sugar) and six molecules of oxygen. The oxygen is released into the air. Almost all of the oxygen we breathe was made this way.

The equation shows the starting materials and the products, but not the many small steps in between. Those steps happen in two connected stages.

## Where it happens: the chloroplast

Photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts. These are small structures found mostly in the cells of leaves. A single leaf cell can hold dozens of them. Each chloroplast contains stacks of flat, disc-shaped sacs called thylakoids. A stack of thylakoids is called a granum. The thick fluid around the stacks is called the stroma.

The thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll, a green pigment. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light very well but reflects green light. That reflected green light is why most leaves look green to us.

## Stage 1: the light-dependent reactions

The first stage happens in the thylakoid membranes and needs light. When chlorophyll absorbs light, the energy excites electrons in the chlorophyll molecules. These high-energy electrons are passed along a chain of proteins in the membrane, called the electron transport chain.

To replace the electrons it has lost, the plant splits water molecules. Splitting water releases oxygen gas as a by-product. The energy from the moving electrons is used to make two energy-carrying molecules: ATP and NADPH. You can think of ATP and NADPH as charged batteries that carry energy to the second stage.

## Stage 2: the Calvin cycle

The second stage happens in the stroma. It does not use light directly, so it is sometimes called the light-independent reactions. It still depends on the ATP and NADPH made in stage 1.

In the Calvin cycle, an enzyme called rubisco attaches carbon dioxide from the air to a five-carbon molecule already in the chloroplast. This step is called carbon fixation, because carbon from a gas is "fixed" into a solid compound. Using energy from ATP and NADPH, the cell then turns these compounds into a three-carbon sugar. Some of this sugar leaves the cycle and is used to build glucose. The rest is recycled to keep the cycle running. It takes six turns of the cycle to fix the six carbon atoms in one glucose molecule.

## What limits the rate

The speed of photosynthesis depends on several limiting factors:

- Light intensity: more light means more energy for stage 1, up to a point.
- Carbon dioxide concentration: more carbon dioxide gives the Calvin cycle more raw material.
- Temperature: the enzymes that run photosynthesis work best in a certain range. Too cold and they slow down. Too hot and they can be damaged.

Whichever factor is in shortest supply sets the overall rate. For example, on a bright, warm day, a greenhouse farmer may add carbon dioxide to the air, because carbon dioxide has become the limiting factor.

## Worked example: measuring the rate

A student places a pondweed plant in water under a lamp and counts the oxygen bubbles it releases. With the lamp 40 cm away, the plant releases 12 bubbles per minute. With the lamp 20 cm away, it releases 30 bubbles per minute. Halving the distance roughly quadruples the light reaching the plant, but the bubble rate only went up 2.5 times. This suggests that at 20 cm, light is no longer the only limiting factor; carbon dioxide or temperature may now be limiting the rate.

## Why it matters

Photosynthesis is the starting point of almost every food chain. The glucose plants make is used for energy in cellular respiration, or stored as starch, or built into cellulose for cell walls. Animals get this energy by eating plants, or by eating animals that ate plants. Photosynthesis also removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which links it to the global carbon cycle and to climate.
